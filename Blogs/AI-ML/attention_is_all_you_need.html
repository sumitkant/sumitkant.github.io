01

The problem

Why replace recurrence at all?

Before 2017, the best language models were Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and their upgraded versions, LSTMs and GRUs. They worked well, but they read a sentence one word at a time, and that made them slow to train.

A 60-second crash course on RNNs

An RNN reads a sentence the way you read with a finger under the words. At each word it updates a small memory, called the hidden state, by mixing the new word with what it remembered so far. After the last word, that memory is supposed to summarise the whole sentence.

  • RNN, Recurrent Neural Network: the same small network is applied at every word, and it passes its memory forward to the next step. "Recurrent" means it keeps feeding its own output back in.
  • LSTM, Long Short-Term Memory (1997): adds gates that decide what to keep, what to forget and what to output, so useful information survives across longer sentences.
  • GRU, Gated Recurrent Unit (2014): a slimmer LSTM with fewer gates and similar results.

An RNN passes a hidden state along a chain. The Transformer replaces the chain with direct links between every pair of words.

The two problems with the chain

  • It is slow. Step 5 cannot start until step 4 finishes. GPUs are fast because they do many things at once, but along a sentence an RNN gives them nothing to do in parallel.
  • It forgets. Information from the first word has to survive every hop to reach the last word. Gates help, but long sentences still blur.

Convolutional models were the other option at the time. They parallelised better, but needed several stacked layers before two distant words could influence each other.

The Transformer's core bet Drop the chain entirely. Let every word directly decide which other words matter to it.
From sequential state propagation
to direct token-to-token communication.
02

Architecture

The Transformer, end to end

This post is about the Transformer as a whole: how its pieces fit together and why each one is there. Attention is only one of those pieces.

The model is still an encoder–decoder, the standard design for translation. The encoder reads the source sentence and turns every token into a context-aware vector. The decoder then writes the translation one token at a time, looking back at the encoder's vectors as it goes.

Encoder · 6 identical layers

Read the input

Each layer: self-attention → add & norm → feed-forward → add & norm.

Decoder · 6 identical layers

Write the output

Each layer: masked self-attention → cross-attention → feed-forward, each followed by add & norm.

The full model, following Figure 1 of the paper. Attention boxes are only part of each layer; every sub-layer also has a residual connection and a LayerNorm.

One sentence through the model

  1. Embed. Each source token becomes a 512-number vector.
  2. Add position. A positional encoding is added so the model knows word order.
  3. Encode. Six encoder layers refine every token's vector using the whole sentence.
  4. Decode. The decoder takes the translation so far, attends to itself (masked) and to the encoder's vectors (cross-attention).
  5. Predict. A linear layer and softmax turn the top decoder vector into a probability for every word in the 37K vocabulary.
  6. Repeat. The chosen token is appended to the output and the decoder runs again.
03

Attention, briefly

Attention: what this paper adds

Each token builds a query, a key and a value. Queries are compared with keys to decide how much of each value to pull in.

$$\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{T}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

I have already worked through the mechanics of attention in a separate post: the four steps of self-attention, softmax, masking, cross-attention and multi-head attention, with hand calculations and PyTorch code. The relevant details are covered here:

Attention · Transformers · PyTorch
Attention in Transformers: From Equation to PyTorch, Checked by Hand

Q, K, V, the softmax, masked self-attention, encoder–decoder attention and multi-head attention, worked through by hand and in code.

Below are only the parts that go beyond that post: the paper's own reasoning and the design choices that are specific to the Transformer.

1 · Why divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\), precisely

The paper gives a short argument (footnote 4). Suppose each component of a query \(q\) and a key \(k\) is random with mean 0 and variance 1. Their dot product is a sum of \(d_k\) such terms:

$$q \cdot k = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathrm{Var}(q \cdot k) = d_k$$

With \(d_k = 64\), the scores have a standard deviation of 8. Softmax over numbers that far apart puts almost all weight on one token, and its gradients vanish. Dividing by \(\sqrt{64} = 8\) brings the scores back to variance 1.

2 · Why dot-product attention and not additive attention

Earlier translation models mostly used additive attention, which scores a query–key pair with a small feed-forward network. The paper picks dot-product attention instead:

Type Score Trade-off
Additive a one-hidden-layer network on \(q\) and \(k\) works well even for large \(d_k\), but slower
Scaled dot-product \(q \cdot k / \sqrt{d_k}\) one matrix multiply, much faster and more memory-efficient on GPUs

The authors note that plain dot-product attention falls behind additive attention for large \(d_k\). The \(\sqrt{d_k}\) scaling is what closes that gap.

3 · Multi-head attention at no extra cost

Instead of one attention over 512 dimensions, the base model runs 8 heads over 64 dimensions each, then concatenates them back to 512. Because each head is 8× smaller, the total compute is about the same as a single full-width head. The model gets eight different views of the sentence for free.

$$h = 8, \qquad d_k = d_v = d_{model}/h = 512/8 = 64$$

4 · Where the three attentions plug into the Transformer

Attention Queries from Keys & values from Mask
Encoder self-attention previous encoder layer previous encoder layer none
Decoder self-attention previous decoder layer previous decoder layer future tokens set to \(-\infty\)
Cross-attention previous decoder layer final encoder output none
04

Architecture beyond attention

The rest of the block

Attention is one of four ingredients in every layer. The other three decide whether a deep stack trains at all, and they hold most of the parameters.

One encoder layer. The token vector travels along the residual stream; each sub-layer computes an update that is added back, then normalized.

Residual connections

Every sub-layer's output is added to its own input: \(x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(x)\). The sub-layer only has to learn a correction, not rebuild the whole representation. Gradients also get a direct path back through the addition, which is what makes a 6-layer encoder plus a 6-layer decoder trainable. To make the addition possible, every sub-layer and embedding outputs exactly \(d_{model} = 512\) numbers.

Layer normalization

After each addition, LayerNorm rescales every token's vector to mean 0 and variance 1, then applies a learned scale and shift. This keeps values from drifting as they pass through layer after layer. The paper places it after the addition:

$$\mathrm{output} = \mathrm{LayerNorm}\big(x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(x)\big)$$
Later models changed this GPT-2 and most models since move LayerNorm before the sub-layer ("pre-LN"), which trains more stably in very deep stacks. The original Transformer is "post-LN".

Position-wise feed-forward network

After attention has mixed information between tokens, each token goes through the same small two-layer network on its own:

$$\mathrm{FFN}(x) = \max(0,\; xW_1 + b_1)\,W_2 + b_2$$
\(W_1\)
expands 512 → 2048
\(\max(0,\cdot)\)
ReLU activation
\(W_2\)
projects 2048 → 512

The weights are shared across positions within a layer but differ from layer to layer. The paper describes it as two convolutions with kernel size 1.

~1.05M

Division of labour Attention moves information between tokens. The feed-forward network processes the information within each token. Most of the parameters are in the second part.

Embeddings and the output layer share weights

The input embedding, the output embedding and the final linear layer before softmax all use the same weight matrix. A word's input vector and the vector used to score it as a prediction are the same. This saves parameters and ties the two meanings together. In the embedding layers, the weights are multiplied by \(\sqrt{d_{model}} \approx 22.6\).

The decoder sees only the past

During training the decoder receives the whole target sentence at once, shifted right by one position, and the attention mask hides future tokens. Each position therefore predicts the next token using only the tokens before it:

decoder input <start>DieKatzesaß
predicts DieKatzesaß<end>

This lets every position train in parallel, while generation still runs one token at a time.

Dropout

Dropout (\(P_{drop} = 0.1\)) is applied to the output of every sub-layer before it is added back, and to the sum of embeddings and positional encodings.

05

Order

Attention alone does not know token order

Self-attention can compare token representations, but by itself it has no built-in notion that one token came before another. The model therefore adds a positional vector to every token embedding.

token embedding + positional encoding = input representation

The paper uses sinusoidal functions at different frequencies:

$$PE_{(pos,\,2i)} = \sin\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{model}}}\right)$$
$$PE_{(pos,\,2i+1)} = \cos\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{model}}}\right)$$
\(pos\)
the token's position in the sequence
\(i\)
the dimension index; each pair of dimensions gets its own frequency
\(d_{model}\)
the embedding size, 512 in the base model

These give each position a deterministic pattern. The authors also tried learned position embeddings and reported almost identical translation quality in their experiment.

Attention tells the model what relates to what.
Positional encoding tells it where each token sits.

The famous comparison

What Table 1 is really saying

Table 1 is not an accuracy table. It compares four layer types on three architectural properties: computational cost, sequential depth and the number of hops needed for distant positions to communicate.

Layer Complexity Sequential ops Max path
Self-attention \(O(n^2 d)\) \(O(1)\) \(O(1)\)
Recurrent \(O(n d^2)\) \(O(n)\) \(O(n)\)
Convolutional \(O(k n d^2)\) \(O(1)\) \(O(\log_k n)\)
Restricted attention \(O(r n d)\) \(O(1)\) \(O(n/r)\)
Question I had while reading

"I did not understand Table 1. Help me understand from scratch."

Sequential operations asks how many computation stages must wait for previous stages. An RNN has roughly \(n\); self-attention can compute all pairwise scores together, so it has constant sequential depth with respect to sequence length.

Maximum path length asks how many computational hops are needed for one token to influence another. In an RNN, two distant positions may require \(O(n)\) hops. In full self-attention, token 1 can directly attend to token \(n\) in one layer: \(O(1)\).

The trade-off: that direct global access requires an \(n \times n\) attention matrix, which creates the quadratic \(n^2\) term.

Why self-attention can be cheaper than recurrence

Compare \(n^2 d\) with \(n d^2\). Cancel \(nd\), and self-attention is cheaper in this simplified comparison when:

$$n < d$$

For \(n = 100\) and \(d = 512\):

Self-attention\(n^2 d\)
5.12M
Recurrent\(n d^2\)
26.2M

Roughly 5.1× fewer operations for self-attention at this sequence length.

The real win Not "attention is always cheaper." The win is parallelism + constant path length, with quadratic sequence-length cost as the trade-off.

Optimization

Adam was used with \(\beta_1 = 0.9\), \(\beta_2 = 0.98\), and \(\epsilon = 10^{-9}\). The learning rate warmed up for 4,000 steps, then decayed approximately with the inverse square root of the step number.

$$\mathrm{lrate} = d_{model}^{-0.5} \cdot \min\!\left(\mathrm{step}^{-0.5},\; \mathrm{step} \cdot \mathrm{warmup}^{-1.5}\right)$$
Base model

~100K steps · 12 hours

Big model

~300K steps · 3.5 days

Regularization

The base model used 0.1 dropout and 0.1 label smoothing.

A useful subtlety Label smoothing can improve BLEU and accuracy while making perplexity worse, because the model is deliberately discouraged from becoming maximally confident in the target label.

BLEU measures overlap between generated translations and reference translations using n-gram matching and a brevity penalty. Higher is better. The paper's important claim was not only strong translation quality, but strong quality with substantially less training computation than several previous systems listed in the comparison.

Question I had while reading

"What is perplexity on page 9?"

Perplexity is a transformation of average cross-entropy loss:

$$\mathrm{PPL} = e^{\text{cross-entropy}}$$

Lower is better. A loose intuition is the model's effective number of plausible next-token choices. So 4.33 is better than 4.92, although the model is not literally choosing uniformly among 4.33 tokens.

These are per-wordpiece perplexities, so they should not be directly compared with word-level perplexities produced under a different tokenization scheme.

Beyond translation

Why the parsing experiment mattered

The paper also applied the Transformer to English constituency parsing. This is a structured prediction task: given a sentence, predict a tree describing grammatical constituents such as noun phrases and verb phrases.

example gold parse structure
(S
  (NP (DT The) (NN cat))
  (VP (VBD sat)
      (PP (IN on)
          (NP (DT the) (NN mat)))))
Training setup WSJ Section 23 F1
Transformer, WSJ only 91.3
Transformer, semi-supervised 92.7
Best result listed in the paper table 93.3
Question I had while reading

"What is F1 score in Table 4?"

F1 measures how well the predicted parse tree recovers the constituents in the gold tree.

$$P = \frac{\text{correct predicted}}{\text{all predicted}}, \qquad R = \frac{\text{correct predicted}}{\text{all gold}}$$
$$F_1 = 2\,\frac{P R}{P + R}$$

If the gold tree contains 100 constituents, the model predicts 95, and 90 are correct, precision is 94.7%, recall is 90%, and F1 is about 92.3.

Question I had while reading

"How do you prepare this ground-truth structure? Is it already in a dataset?"

Yes. The experiment uses the Penn Treebank, a pre-existing manually annotated linguistic dataset. The paper does not build the gold trees from scratch.

  1. Start with raw sentences, largely from Wall Street Journal text.
  2. Assign part-of-speech labels and phrase-structure annotations.
  3. Human annotators create and/or correct parse trees under a common guideline.
  4. Run quality-control and consistency checks.
  5. Split the treebank into train, development and test sections.

For this experiment, WSJ Section 22 was used for development and Section 23 for final evaluation. The WSJ-only training set contains roughly 40K sentences.

10

What was actually new

The breakthrough was the architecture, not a single ingredient

Encoder–decoder models already existed. Attention already existed. Residual connections, layer normalization and feed-forward networks already existed. Positional representations also had precedent.

The important step was combining these ideas into a practical sequence model where attention replaces recurrent and convolutional sequence-processing layers, then showing that the design was both computationally attractive and empirically strong.

"How do we build a better recurrent model?"
became "How far can we scale attention?"

Six ideas to keep

  1. The Transformer is an encoder–decoder built from four parts repeated in every layer: attention, feed-forward, residual addition and LayerNorm.
  2. Attention moves information between tokens; the feed-forward network processes each token on its own and holds about two-thirds of each layer's weights.
  3. Residual connections and LayerNorm keep a 512-d residual stream that every sub-layer reads from and adds to. That is what makes a deep stack trainable.
  4. Positional encoding is necessary because attention alone does not encode order.
  5. The decoder is the same block plus masking and cross-attention; shifting the outputs right lets it train on all positions in parallel.
  6. The whole design trades the RNN's sequential chain for parallel computation and one-hop paths, at a quadratic cost in sequence length.
The simplest mental model Every token repeatedly asks the rest of the sequence, "What information should I pull from you?"
